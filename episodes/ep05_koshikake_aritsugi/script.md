# Episode 5 — The First Joint: Koshikake Ari-tsugi · final script

Runtime 4:53.3 · 544 words · timestamps are narration start–end in the final cut.


## 1. Cold open

**0:00.8–0:09.2** (N01) Two sills meet end to end. One piece drops in from above, and now they can't slide apart.

**0:09.6–0:14.4** (N02) This is our first joint: the koshikake ari-tsugi.


## 2. A splice: tsugite

**0:20.1–0:32.7** (N03) It's a splice, a tsugite: a joint that joins two members end to end, in line. A joint between members meeting at an angle is called a shiguchi.

**0:36.0–0:59.1** (N04) Ari is the dovetail: a shape that flares out like a dove's tail. Koshikake is the seat: the ledge the upper piece sits on. So this is a dovetail splice, with a seat. You'll also see it called shikimen ari-tsugi; shikimen is another word for the seat.


## 3. Where it goes

**1:02.9–1:13.6** (N05) It's used mainly to splice sills, the timbers that sit on the foundation, and also ōbiki, the ground-floor beams that carry the floor joists.

**1:14.0–1:40.4** (N06) The building specification behind Japan's Flat 35 home loans names it as one of two splices for sills, and says to keep the splice away from posts and underfloor vents. It asks for sills at least a hundred and five millimeters square. The government's public-works specification adds: put the anchor bolt in the upper piece, next to the joint.


## 4. The parts

**1:44.1–1:54.2** (N07) There are two pieces. The male piece, ogi, carries the dovetail. The female piece, megi, carries the socket and the seat.

**2:07.5–2:23.1** (N08) The dovetail and its socket take roughly the upper half of the sill. The seat takes the lower half. The male piece's lower half is cut back, so it sits on that seat.

**1:54.6–2:03.3** (N09) Carpenters name the parts: the wide head, the narrow neck, and the shoulder, where the neck meets the body.


## 5. How it goes together

**2:26.4–2:46.4** (N10) The male piece goes in from above. The flare stops it coming in from the end, and the socket walls stop it coming in from the side. So it drops straight down: the dovetail settles into its socket, and the cut-back underside lands on the seat.

**2:46.8–2:58.7** (N11) Once it's down, the flare hooks the two pieces together, so they can't simply slide apart along their length. And the male piece rests on the seat.


## 6. Proportions

**2:59.5–3:16.7** (N12) How big? Proportions vary. One engineering study works within these ranges: a neck a quarter to a third of the sill's width, and a dovetail a quarter to a half of the width long. Our drawing sits inside both.

**3:17.1–3:25.5** (N13) In our drawing, each side slopes about one in five, in line with what the test reports describe for these joints.

**3:25.9–3:35.1** (N14) In one training course, the slope of the sides is set out with the sashigane itself, using the width of its blade.


## 7. Limits

**3:39.3–4:04.2** (N15) Be clear about what it isn't. The hook holds only a modest pull, so it's counted as weak in tension. One test report treats it almost as a hinge, with very little resistance to bending. In a house, an anchor bolt holds the upper piece down beside the joint. Never put the bolt in the female piece, or inside the joint itself.

**4:04.6–4:19.1** (N16) And it fails suddenly: the female piece can split at the notch, or the dovetail can shear off. Keep knots out of the joint, and don't expect a longer dovetail to make it stronger.


## 8. Next time

**4:22.8–4:28.1** (N17) Next time, we'll mark it out and cut it, step by step.


## 9. Recap

**4:29.1–4:40.6** (N18) A dovetail above, a seat below. The upper piece, the male, drops in from above. And the splice goes away from posts and vents.
