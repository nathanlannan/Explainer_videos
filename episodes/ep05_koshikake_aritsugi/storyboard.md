Once it's down, the flare hooks the two pieces together, so they can't simply slide apart along their length. And the male piece rests on the seat. |
| S09 Proportions | 2:59.2 | 39.7 s | 2d | Plan to scale: the engineering ranges for neck and length as bands; our drawing inside; slope 1:5; schematic of the blade-width method. Study hold 3.5 s. | How big? Proportions vary. One engineering study works within these ranges: a neck a quarter to a third of the sill's width, and a dovetail a quarter to a half of the width long. Our drawing sits inside both. In our drawing, each side slopes about one in five, in line with what the test reports describe for these joints. In one training course, the slope of the sides is set out with the sashigane itself, using the width of its blade. |
| S10 What it isn't | 3:39.0 | 43.5 s | 2d | Modest pull; almost a hinge; anchor bolt in the upper piece (✕ not in the female, not inside the joint); failure modes; keep knots out. Study hold 3.0 s. | Be clear about what it isn't. The hook holds only a modest pull, so it's counted as weak in tension. One test report treats it almost as a hinge, with very little resistance to bending. In a house, an anchor bolt holds the upper piece down beside the joint. Never put the bolt in the female piece, or inside the joint itself. And it fails suddenly: the female piece can split at the notch, or the dovetail can shear off. Keep knots out of the joint, and don't expect a longer dovetail to make it stronger. |
