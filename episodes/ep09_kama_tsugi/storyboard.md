| S04 The parts | 0:59.7 | 50.2 s | threeQ | Exploded: male 男木 above female 女木; head 鎌, neck 首, seat 腰掛. The male drops in; ghost slides ✕ (fit-checked); a neutral lift arrow. Study hold 3.5 s. | Like the dovetail splice in episode five, there's a male piece, the upper one, and a female, the lower one. The head and its socket take about the upper half of the depth. The lower half is the seat, the koshikake. A narrow neck carries a wider head at its end. On screen are the names a glossary gives each part. It goes together like the dovetail. The male drops straight down and rests on the seat. Pull along the timbers, and the flared back of the head bears against the socket. In our model, it can't slide sideways either. Nothing stops it lifting, though. |
| S05 Proportions and strength | 1:49.9 | 80.6 s | 2d | Plans to scale: the gooseneck (test-specimen sizes, 120 mm) beside Episode 5's dovetail drawing (105 mm); attributed test loads; stress near the neck; failure modes; 'often treated as a pin'. Study hold 3.5 s. | The gooseneck's proportions on screen come from one test series on hundred-and-twenty millimeter timbers. No carpentry source we found gives standard sizes. In these drawings, the gooseneck reaches much further than our dovetail: a long neck, then a head. In one national test database, the gooseneck specimens held roughly two to three times the pull of the dovetail specimens. The timber grades and joint sizes differed, so take that as a rough guide, not a rule. And a longer gooseneck isn't simply stronger. Researchers found stress gathers near the neck, and one study found the depth of the timbers mattered more than the length of the splice. It often ends in a sudden, brittle failure: the head or neck shears off. Sometimes the flared edges crush into the socket first. The test database notes it's often treated as a pin in design: it holds against a pull, but resists bending very little. |
| S06 Where it goes | 3:10.5 | 53.9 s | 2d | Flat 35: sills, eaves plates, purlins (ari or kama); 胴差し (kama or another splice, not ari); roof beams about 120 deep and one floor-beam option with straps and bolts; purlins two nails. Sill anchor bolt in the upper piece (public-works spec). Study hold 3.5 s. | The Flat 35 house specification offers it, alongside the dovetail splice, for sills, eaves plates and purlins. For the beams in the outer walls at second-floor level, it offers the gooseneck or another splice, but not the dovetail. For roof beams, the spec allows it only where the beam is about a hundred and twenty millimeters deep, and then adds steel straps on both faces, bolted through. One option for second-floor floor beams does the same. On purlins, two nails. On a sill, as in episode five, the public-works specification puts the anchor bolt in the upper piece, beside the splice. |
