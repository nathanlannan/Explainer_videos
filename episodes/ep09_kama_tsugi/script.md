# Episode 9 — The Gooseneck: Koshikake Kama-tsugi · final script

Runtime 5:01.5 · 540 words · timestamps are narration start–end in the final cut.


## 1. Cold open

**0:00.8–0:15.1** (N01) Two timbers meet end to end. The male piece drops in from above: a head on a narrow neck slides down into a socket, and the timber comes to rest on a seat.

**0:15.5–0:22.4** (N02) This is the koshikake kama-tsugi. In English, it's often called a gooseneck joint.


## 2. The name

**0:27.9–0:48.3** (N03) Two dictionaries read it kama-tsugi, and they also list kama alone as a short form. On its own, kama means a sickle. Two sources compare the tenon to a kamakubi: a snake raising its head on a neck curved like a sickle.

**0:48.7–0:56.3** (N04) Gooseneck is an English name, not a translation. The Japanese name says nothing about geese.


## 3. The parts

**1:00.0–1:16.3** (N05) Like the dovetail splice in episode five, there's a male piece, the upper one, and a female, the lower one. The head and its socket take about the upper half of the depth. The lower half is the seat, the koshikake.

**1:16.7–1:25.2** (N06) A narrow neck carries a wider head at its end. On screen are the names a glossary gives each part.

**1:25.6–1:46.0** (N07) It goes together like the dovetail. The male drops straight down and rests on the seat. Pull along the timbers, and the flared back of the head bears against the socket. In our model, it can't slide sideways either. Nothing stops it lifting, though.


## 4. Proportions and strength

**1:50.2–2:03.7** (N08) The gooseneck's proportions on screen come from one test series on hundred-and-twenty millimeter timbers. No carpentry source we found gives standard sizes.

**2:04.1–2:28.0** (N09) In these drawings, the gooseneck reaches much further than our dovetail: a long neck, then a head. In one national test database, the gooseneck specimens held roughly two to three times the pull of the dovetail specimens. The timber grades and joint sizes differed, so take that as a rough guide, not a rule.

**2:28.4–2:44.3** (N10) And a longer gooseneck isn't simply stronger. Researchers found stress gathers near the neck, and one study found the depth of the timbers mattered more than the length of the splice.

**2:44.7–3:06.6** (N11) It often ends in a sudden, brittle failure: the head or neck shears off. Sometimes the flared edges crush into the socket first. The test database notes it's often treated as a pin in design: it holds against a pull, but resists bending very little.


## 5. Where it goes

**3:10.8–3:30.8** (N12) The Flat 35 house specification offers it, alongside the dovetail splice, for sills, eaves plates and purlins. For the beams in the outer walls at second-floor level, it offers the gooseneck or another splice, but not the dovetail.

**3:31.2–3:50.2** (N13) For roof beams, the spec allows it only where the beam is about a hundred and twenty millimeters deep, and then adds steel straps on both faces, bolted through. One option for second-floor floor beams does the same. On purlins, two nails.

**3:50.6–4:00.5** (N14) On a sill, as in episode five, the public-works specification puts the anchor bolt in the upper piece, beside the splice.


## 6. Limits

**4:04.7–4:16.8** (N15) A short stub tenon is sometimes added under the seat. Its job is to resist twisting. In one test series, it added no pulling strength.

**4:17.2–4:27.1** (N16) And knots or grain run-out at the splice weaken it. The test database advises a generous safety margin, or added hardware.


## 7. Next time

**4:30.8–4:34.5** (N17) Next time: marking out and cutting the gooseneck.


## 8. Recap

**4:35.5–4:48.8** (N18) The koshikake kama-tsugi: a head on a neck, dropped onto a seat. It holds against a pull, but engineers often model it as a pin.
