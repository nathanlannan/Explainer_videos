| S03 The board plan | 0:25.0 | 52.1 s | 2d | A plywood board; the plan drawn in ink: grid lines, posts at crossings, sills and beams between them; 910 mm spacing; grid lines through post centres. Study hold 3.5 s. | This is an itazu, a board plan. It's the carpenter's own drawing of the building, made on a thin board, today often plywood, and drawn from the architect's plans. It goes by other names too: zuita, or teita. It is not a detailed drawing. It works like a floor plan and the framing plans combined: where every post stands, and which beams and sills run between them. The board is ruled into a grid. The lines are usually three shaku apart, about nine hundred ten millimeters, and the posts stand at the crossings. Each grid line runs through the centers of the posts on it. |
| S04 Banzuke addresses | 1:17.1 | 54.7 s | 2d | Iroha across the top, numerals down the side; the corner post い一 and its neighbour ろ一 highlighted; an inset shows another carpenter's layout. Study hold 3.5 s. | Then every grid line gets a name. This system is called banzuke. In its classic form, one direction is labeled with the old Japanese syllable order, i, ro, ha, ni. The other is numbered, one, two, three. Now every crossing has an address. The corner post here is i-no-ichi, written with two characters. The next one along is ro-no-ichi. One common layout runs the syllables across the board and counts the numbers down from the top. But which way the axes go, and which corner starts, varies by region and by carpenter. Other numbering systems exist too. |
| S05 Onto the timber | 2:11.9 | 37.4 s | threeQ | A post being marked out: its address い一 written by hand near the bottom; story stick and centre line called out; the address sits where the floor will hide it. Study hold 3.0 s. | Now the plan comes off the board. Marking out each member, the carpenter checks the board and writes that member's address on it, by hand. The address says where the piece goes; the story stick gives its heights; the center line gives the reference. The address goes where it will be hidden once the frame is built. On a tenon, say, or on the part of a post that ends up below the floor. |
