## 2. Meet the sashigane

**0:21.6–0:32.8** (N03) This is a sashigane, the Japanese carpenter's square. It's a thin L of steel, today usually stainless, marked as a ruler along its edges.

**0:33.2–0:46.3** (N04) The long arm is the nagate. The short arm is the tsumate. The common size is scaled to five hundred millimeters on the long arm, and two hundred fifty on the short.

**0:47.2–1:06.8** (N05) Beside it, the Western framing square: twenty-four inches by sixteen, with a two-inch blade. Both are squares and rulers at once. But the sashigane is only about fifteen millimeters wide, and only about one and a half millimeters thick.

**1:09.1–1:22.4** (N06) That thinness is deliberate. The blade flexes and springs back, so the carpenter can bend it slightly with their fingers and press it flat against the wood, right where the line goes.

**1:23.2–1:34.5** (N07) Used as a square, it carried the lines across the posts in the last episode. How to hold it? That comes next time, with the rest of the marking kit.


## 3. Front and back scales

**1:35.4–1:47.0** (N08) The front carries ordinary measurements, the omote-me. Today that's usually millimeters. Some squares carry the traditional shaku units instead.

**1:47.7–2:03.4** (N09) Turn it over, and the back carries a second scale, the ura-me. Its marks are spaced wider. Each unit is a front unit times the square root of two: about one point four one four.

**2:07.7–2:22.1** (N10) Why would anyone want a stretched ruler? Because of one shape that is everywhere in timber: the square. A square's diagonal is always its side times the square root of two.

**2:22.5–2:41.9** (N11) So measure a diagonal with the back scale, and the number you read is the side. A square one hundred millimeters on a side has a diagonal of one hundred forty-one point four. On the back scale, that same length reads one hundred.


## 4. The log: largest square beam

**2:46.7–3:03.5** (N12) Back to the log. Measure at the small end, because a log tapers and the small end limits the beam. The biggest square you can cut there has its corners on the circle, so its diagonal is the log's diameter.

**3:03.9–3:20.5** (N13) Lay the back scale across the small end, straight through the center, and read it at the far edge. Two hundred twelve. That's the side of the largest square beam this log can give: two hundred twelve millimeters.

**3:20.9–3:30.5** (N14) No calculator, no pencil arithmetic, no chance to slip a digit. The arithmetic is built into the tool.

**3:35.7–3:48.2** (N15) The same scale is used in laying out roofs, where hips run on diagonals. That craft has its own name, kikujutsu, and it's beyond this series.

**3:49.0–4:02.0** (N16) Some squares add one more scale, the marume. Its units are a front unit divided by pi. Lay it across a log's diameter, and it reads the circumference.


## 5. Same idea, two traditions

**4:04.3–4:19.2** (N17) The framing square plays the same trick in its own way. Its rafter and board-measure tables put the arithmetic on the steel too. Two traditions, one idea: let the tool do the math.

**4:20.1–4:33.0** (N18) Next time, the rest of the marking kit: the ink line and the ink pen, and the gauges that scribe parallel lines, each beside its Western cousin.


## 6. Recap

**4:33.9–4:45.2** (N19) A thin, springy square that is also a calculator. The front measures lengths. The back measures diagonals, and reads you the side.
