## 2. Chisels: nomi

**0:24.0–0:35.0** (N03) Japanese chisels, nomi, come in two families. Tataki-nomi are struck with a hammer. Tsuki-nomi are pushed by hand, with no hammer.

**0:35.4–0:44.2** (N04) A struck chisel has an iron hoop fitted around the end of its handle. This hoop is called the katsura.

**0:48.4–1:05.4** (N05) The general bench chisel, struck with a hammer, is the oire-nomi. You'll also see it read oiire, or written ōire. For deep mortises there is the mukōmachi-nomi, with a thick blade.


## 3. The blade

**1:09.6–1:43.5** (N06) Now look at the blade itself. A woodworking chisel is bevelled on one side only. And it's made of two layers: a thin layer of hard steel, hagane, forge-welded to a thicker body of softer iron, jigane. The softer iron supports the brittle steel and absorbs shock, so the steel doesn't break. One Japanese blade maker puts it simply: foreign blades are usually one piece of steel; Japanese blades are laminated.

**1:43.9–1:57.8** (N07) The back of the blade has a shallow hollow, the urasuki. It makes the back easier to flatten. The hollow is in the back, not the bevel: the chisel is not hollow-ground.


## 4. The hammer: gennō

**2:02.0–2:09.5** (N08) The hammer is the gennō. One face is flat; the other is slightly domed.

**2:09.9–2:24.0** (N09) Driving a nail, you strike with the flat face, then give the last blows with the domed face, so the surface isn't marked. The domed face is the kigoroshi-men: literally, the wood-killing face.


## 5. The plane: kanna

**2:27.7–2:35.3** (N10) The plane is the kanna. Like the saw, it's pulled toward you. Western and Chinese planes are pushed.

**2:35.7–2:55.2** (N11) The blade sits in a wooden block, the dai, usually of oak. In the national skills-test manual, the blade is set by tapping with a hammer: on the blade's head to set it deeper, and on the corners of the block's head end to draw it back.

**2:57.6–3:14.7** (N12) A two-blade plane, nimai-ganna, adds a second iron behind the blade: the chip breaker, uragane. It helps stop tear-out. Like the ryōba, it is a Meiji-era development, not an ancient one.


## 6. Western cousins

**3:18.9–3:32.8** (N13) If you work with Western tools, the jobs are the same: bench chisels, a hammer or mallet, a bench plane. The plane is the clearest difference: a Western plane is pushed.


## 7. Sharp edges

**3:35.6–3:53.0** (N14) Chisels and planes depend on a sharp edge. The skills-test manual warns that a dull tool invites injury, and never to point a chisel's edge at yourself or anyone else. It also says to sharpen chisels and planes well after use.


## 8. Next time

**3:55.4–4:00.3** (N15) Next time, our first joint: the koshikake ari-tsugi.


## 9. Recap and a note

**4:01.3–4:12.0** (N16) Strike the chisel, or push it. Tap the blade into its block. Pull the plane. And keep every edge sharp.

**4:21.9–4:31.9** (N17) One note: sharp-tool technique, and how to use these tools safely, is best learned from a teacher, at a bench, in person.
