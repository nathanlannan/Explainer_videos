| S04 The parts | 1:03.2 | 87.7 s | threeQ | Exploded: lower and upper pieces (same shape, turned end for end); half width over the splice; ago step and mechigai tongues; the upper drops in; slope inset; pegs from opposite sides; fit-check demo. Study hold 3.5 s. | Unlike the gooseneck, the two pieces aren't different shapes. Both are the same shape, turned end for end, though one training course warns that the slope runs the other way on the upper piece. They're called the lower piece and the upper piece: the lower piece is set first, and the upper piece is lowered onto it. Over the splice, each piece keeps about half the width, but its full depth. Seen from above, the joint line runs along the beam, with a step at the middle, the ago, and a small tongue at each end, the mechigai. The step at the middle has a slight slope. As the upper piece is lowered, the slope draws the two pieces together along the beam, until the end shoulders close up tight. Then two pegs go in through the side faces, from opposite sides. They stop the joint lifting back out. It's the slope, not the pegs, that the sources credit with drawing it tight. In our model, without the pegs it can't be pulled apart or pushed sideways, but it can lift straight out. With the pegs in, it can't. |
| S05 Where it goes | 2:30.9 | 40.9 s | 2d | Flat 35 clauses; no hardware named; the lower piece cantilevered past the post with the splice beyond it (schematic). Study hold 3.5 s. | The Flat 35 specification offers it for those outer-wall beams, for eaves plates, and for one option on second-floor floor beams. For roof beams it's the first choice; the gooseneck is allowed only where the beam is about a hundred and twenty millimeters deep, and then with steel straps and bolts. None of those clauses names hardware for this splice. As in each of them, it's placed off the post, a short way out along the beam. |
| S06 Length and strength | 3:11.8 | 83.5 s | 2d | Length rules (attributed); our model's proportions; tension bars vs the gooseneck (same lab); depth over length; bending; slow by hand. Study hold 3.5 s. | How long should it be? A carpenters' training course says about three times the depth gives the most strength, and a joinery handbook gives three to four times for beams a hundred and five or a hundred and twenty millimeters deep. One test database suggests one and a half to three times. Our model uses one laboratory's test proportions: a beam a hundred and twenty millimeters wide and a hundred and eighty deep, with a splice twice the depth. In that lab's tests, at the same depth, these splices held two to three and a half times the pull of its gooseneck specimens, though they were also much longer. Again, the depth of the beam mattered more than the length of the splice. It resists bending better than the gooseneck, but only partly. The test database notes it's often treated as a pin in design. And it's slow to cut by hand, because the two pieces are fitted against each other. One architect's guide reports one to four splices per person per day. |
