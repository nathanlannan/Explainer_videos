The upper timber gets a groove across its underside. The lower timber is cut away left and right, leaving its middle standing. The upper timber comes down. The standing middle fits up into the groove, and the cut-away sides take the rest. In our model, neither timber can now slide along the other. In the model, nothing stops it lifting, though. Seen from the end of the lower timber, its top steps down on both sides. The groove in the timber above fits over the raised middle. How deep are the cuts? | S05 Not a half-lap | 2:10.3 | 30.8 s | threeQ | Side by side: aigaki 相欠き (each half depth, tops flush) and the watari-ago (shallow cuts, different heights). Study hold 3.5 s. | It isn't a half-lap. In a half-lap, aigaki, each timber is cut to half its depth, and their faces finish flush. In the watari-ago, the cuts are shallow, the timbers sit at different heights, and the uncut middle locks into the groove. The dictionary notes it's used where two timbers cross at slightly different heights. |
| S06 Where it goes | 2:41.1 | 62.3 s | 2d | Text panel (no stacking drawn): roof beam ↔ eaves plate per Flat 35: かぶとあり掛け or 渡りあご, with a 羽子板ボルト; flush tops → 大入れあり掛け (not a crossing joint); other uses; 京呂組 (JAANUS). Study hold 3.5 s. | Where is it used? Where a roof beam meets the eaves plate, the Flat 35 specification offers two choices: a dovetail called kabuto-arikake, or the watari-ago. Either one is fastened with a plate bolt. Where the tops of the two are to finish flush, it calls for a different joint: a housed dovetail, which isn't a crossing joint at all. The same specification uses it where second-floor beams rest on another horizontal member, and where log roof beams cross a support, there held with staples. And according to a Japanese architecture glossary, one traditional framing style, which sets the roof beam on top of the eaves plate, uses this joint or the dovetail there. |
| S07 Holding it together | 3:43.4 | 62.0 s | 2d | Art. 47 card and the spec's hardware; a generic plate bolt, not mounted on the joint; a beam over two supports with a mid-span underside notch ✕ (Art. 44) and a neutral marker where one timber rests on the other. Study hold 3.0 s. | Japan's building regulations require the structurally important joints of a timber frame to be fastened, with bolts, staples, pegs or another approved method, so they carry their loads. Depending on where it's used, the specification pairs this joint with a plate bolt, staples, nails or other bolts. A plate bolt is a steel plate with a bolt welded to it. The plate is fixed to one member, and the bolt passes through another and is tightened with a nut. The regulations also forbid notching the underside of a beam, or other horizontal member, near the middle of its span where that would weaken it. In the uses above, the joint is cut where one timber rests on the other, not out in the open span. |
