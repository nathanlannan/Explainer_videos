# Episode 8 — Beam Over Beam: Watari-ago · final script

Runtime 5:10.7 · 552 words · timestamps are narration start–end in the final cut.


## 1. Cold open

**0:00.8–0:11.6** (N01) One timber comes down across another, at right angles. Both have been cut, so the two settle into each other and interlock.

**0:12.0–0:15.9** (N02) This is the watari-ago, a crossing joint.


## 2. The name

**0:21.4–0:35.7** (N03) In episode five, a splice joined timbers end to end. The other family, shiguchi, joins timbers that meet at an angle, and the watari-ago is one of them.

**0:36.1–0:55.7** (N04) As an everyday word, watari can mean a crossing. The second kanji here is read ago, jaw; on its own it can also be read era, gills. The Flat 35 house specification writes that part in kana.

**0:56.1–1:02.9** (N05) Which part of the joint is the jaw? None of our sources say, so we won't guess.


## 3. Both timbers are cut

**1:06.6–1:21.1** (N06) A Japanese dictionary describes it like this. The upper timber gets a groove across its underside. The lower timber is cut away left and right, leaving its middle standing.

**1:21.5–1:39.9** (N07) The upper timber comes down. The standing middle fits up into the groove, and the cut-away sides take the rest. In our model, neither timber can now slide along the other. In the model, nothing stops it lifting, though.

**1:40.3–1:51.7** (N08) Seen from the end of the lower timber, its top steps down on both sides. The groove in the timber above fits over the raised middle.

**1:52.1–2:06.3** (N09) How deep are the cuts? No modern specification we found gives a depth, and an architecture dictionary simply calls them shallow. The depths drawn here are only for illustration.


## 4. Not a half-lap

**2:10.6–2:21.5** (N10) It isn't a half-lap. In a half-lap, aigaki, each timber is cut to half its depth, and their faces finish flush.

**2:21.9–2:37.2** (N11) In the watari-ago, the cuts are shallow, the timbers sit at different heights, and the uncut middle locks into the groove. The dictionary notes it's used where two timbers cross at slightly different heights.


## 5. Where it goes

**2:41.4–2:59.3** (N12) Where is it used? Where a roof beam meets the eaves plate, the Flat 35 specification offers two choices: a dovetail called kabuto-arikake, or the watari-ago. Either one is fastened with a plate bolt.

**2:59.7–3:10.7** (N13) Where the tops of the two are to finish flush, it calls for a different joint: a housed dovetail, which isn't a crossing joint at all.

**3:11.1–3:39.5** (N14) The same specification uses it where second-floor beams rest on another horizontal member, and where log roof beams cross a support, there held with staples. And according to a Japanese architecture glossary, one traditional framing style, which sets the roof beam on top of the eaves plate, uses this joint or the dovetail there.


## 6. Holding it together

**3:43.7–4:09.0** (N15) Japan's building regulations require the structurally important joints of a timber frame to be fastened, with bolts, staples, pegs or another approved method, so they carry their loads. Depending on where it's used, the specification pairs this joint with a plate bolt, staples, nails or other bolts.

**4:09.4–4:22.2** (N16) A plate bolt is a steel plate with a bolt welded to it. The plate is fixed to one member, and the bolt passes through another and is tightened with a nut.

**4:22.6–4:42.0** (N17) The regulations also forbid notching the underside of a beam, or other horizontal member, near the middle of its span where that would weaken it. In the uses above, the joint is cut where one timber rests on the other, not out in the open span.
