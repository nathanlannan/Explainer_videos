# Episode 2 · Part 2 — Ink Line, Pen and Gauges · final script

## 1. Cold open

**0:00.6–0:12.8** (N01) Three meters of timber, and one line down its whole length. No straightedge that long, no pencil. Just a string, and a snap.

**0:14.7–0:20.9** (N02) Every kind of line on a timber has its own tool. This episode, we meet them.


## 2. The ink line

**0:26.0–0:50.8** (N03) The string comes from a sumitsubo, the carpenter's ink line. At the front is a small pond, the ike, packed with silk floss soaked in ink. Behind it, a reel holds the line. At the line's end is a pin, the karuko. Traditional pots were carved from mulberry; zelkova is common today.

**0:54.9–1:04.0** (N04) Push the pin into one end of the mark. Draw the line out through the inked floss, and pull it taut to the other end.

**1:04.3–1:17.2** (N05) Lift it straight up, and let it go. The line slaps down and leaves a straight ink line, even across a slightly uneven surface.

**1:18.0–1:30.6** (N06) A Western chalk line works the same way. The difference is what's left behind: chalk wipes off easily. Ink pots come in sizes from about eighteen to thirty centimeters long.


## 3. The ink pen

**1:33.4–1:51.0** (N07) For everything shorter, there's the sumisashi, the ink pen. It's a strip of bamboo, flattened. One end is cut on a slant and split into fine slits, so it holds ink and draws a clean line.

**1:51.3–2:03.1** (N08) The other end is rounded and crushed into a little brush, for writing marks and words. The notes on the story stick in the first episode were written this way.


## 4. Squaring with the sashigane

**2:03.9–2:13.0** (N09) Now, the promise from last time: how to square a line with a flat steel square that has no thick stock.

**2:13.3–2:29.3** (N10) One arm sticks out past the edge of the board, its inner edge pressed against the side, held by your fingers near the middle of that arm. The other arm lies across the face, and you draw along it.

**2:29.7–2:45.3** (N11) Simply laying the square on top of the board doesn't work: nothing holds it square to the edge. The hooked edge is your reference, so it must be straight and true. And which arm hooks the edge varies; you'll see both.


## 5. The marking gauge

**2:50.0–3:13.9** (N12) For lines parallel to an edge, there's the kebiki, the marking gauge. A fence slides along a planed edge, and a small blade at the end of the beam, sometimes a pin, scores a line. Set the distance once, and the gauge repeats it on every piece, just as the story stick repeats lengths.

**3:14.6–3:28.2** (N13) Because it usually cuts with a blade, it's closest to a Western cutting gauge, not a pin gauge. A pin tends to tear the fibers across the grain. A blade slices them.

**3:32.4–3:43.1** (N14) There are many kinds. The sujikebiki just scores lines. The warikebiki cuts deeper, to split thin boards to width.


## 6. The marking knife

**3:46.4–3:58.5** (N15) And for the finest lines, a marking knife: the shiragaki, also called shirabiki. Most are flat on the back and beveled on one side.

**3:58.8–4:08.5** (N16) A knife line is a fine cut, not just a mark on the surface. When and how to use one comes with the cutting tools.


## 7. Next

**4:09.3–4:16.2** (N17) Next time, we go back to the board plan, and follow the lines from the plan onto the timber.


## 8. Recap

**4:17.2–4:31.8** (N18) Snap the long lines. Write with the pen. Square with the sashigane, gauge the parallels, and knife the finest. Every line has its tool.
