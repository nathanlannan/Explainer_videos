Study hold 2.0 s. | A Western chalk line works the same way. The difference is what's left behind: chalk wipes off easily. Ink pots come in sizes from about eighteen to thirty centimeters long. |
| S06 The ink pen | 1:33.1 | 30.4 s | threeQ | The sumisashi: flattened bamboo; slanted split end draws a line; rounded crushed end writes a note. | For everything shorter, there's the sumisashi, the ink pen. It's a strip of bamboo, flattened. One end is cut on a slant and split into fine slits, so it holds ink and draws a clean line. The other end is rounded and crushed into a little brush, for writing marks and words. The notes on the story stick in the first episode were written this way. |
| S07 Squaring with the sashigane | 2:03.6 | 46.1 s | threeQ | One arm hooked against the board's edge, held near its middle; the other arm across the face; the line drawn along it. Then: laid on top ✕; and the two arm choices. Study hold 4.0 s. | Now, the promise from last time: how to square a line with a flat steel square that has no thick stock. One arm sticks out past the edge of the board, its inner edge pressed against the side, held by your fingers near the middle of that arm. The other arm lies across the face, and you draw along it. Simply laying the square on top of the board doesn't work: nothing holds it square to the edge. The hooked edge is your reference, so it must be straight and true. And which arm hooks the edge varies; you'll see both. |
| S08 The marking gauge | 2:49.7 | 24.6 s | threeQ | A kebiki slides along the board's edge, its blade scoring a parallel line; labels fence, beam, blade. | For lines parallel to an edge, there's the kebiki, the marking gauge. A fence slides along a planed edge, and a small blade at the end of the beam, sometimes a pin, scores a line. Set the distance once, and the gauge repeats it on every piece, just as the story stick repeats lengths. |
| S09 Pin vs blade | 3:14.3 | 17.9 s | 2d | Magnified fibers across the grain: a pin tears them; a blade slices them. Study hold 3.5 s. | Because it usually cuts with a blade, it's closest to a Western cutting gauge, not a pin gauge. A pin tends to tear the fibers across the grain. A blade slices them. |
| S10 Kinds of kebiki | 3:32.1 | 14.0 s | 2d | Names only: sujikebiki 筋罫引 (scores lines), warikebiki 割罫引 (cuts thin boards to width). Study hold 2.5 s. | There are many kinds. The sujikebiki just scores lines. The warikebiki cuts deeper, to split thin boards to width. |
| S11 The marking knife | 3:46.1 | 22.9 s | threeQ | A marking knife (flat back, one bevel) cuts a fine line across a board along the square. | And for the finest lines, a marking knife: the shiragaki, also called shirabiki. Most are flat on the back and beveled on one side. A knife line is a fine cut, not just a mark on the surface. When and how to use one comes with the cutting tools. |
