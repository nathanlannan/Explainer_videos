# Episode 7 — Mortise and Tenon: Hozo · final script

Runtime 5:14.2 · 562 words · timestamps are narration start–end in the final cut.


## 1. Cold open

**0:00.8–0:13.8** (N01) A post comes down onto a sill. A tongue on its end slides into a hole, and a square pin is driven across, pulling the two tight together.

**0:14.2–0:20.6** (N02) This is the mortise and tenon: in Japanese, hozo and hozo-ana.


## 2. Hozo and hozo-ana

**0:26.1–0:37.9** (N03) Hozo is the tenon, the tongue. Hozo-ana, literally tenon hole, is the mortise. Dictionaries note an older reading, hoso.

**0:38.3–0:56.6** (N04) There are many named kinds. A stepped tenon, the jūhozo, or stacked tenon, for example, goes at the top of a post where a wall plate and a beam stack up. This episode looks at the joint where a post meets a sill.


## 3. Post to sill: the options

**0:59.8–1:18.4** (N05) For posts outside the bearing walls, the Flat 35 house specification lists two families of options. A short tenon, held with a metal plate, bracket or staple. Or a long tenon, pinned with a komisen.

**1:18.8–1:28.6** (N06) At an outside corner, the corner post can take the ōgi-hozo, a fan tenon with a trapezoid section, again with hardware.


## 4. The short tenon

**1:32.3–1:48.4** (N07) How big is a short tenon? The test standard for Japan's certified joint hardware uses one on a hundred-and-five millimeter post and sill: thirty thick, eighty-five wide, fifty deep.

**1:48.8–2:06.5** (N08) In a bearing wall, Japan's standard detail drawings give a plain short tenon a joint rating of zero: it isn't counted on to stop the post pulling out. That job needs rated hardware, or a pinned long tenon.


## 5. The long tenon and the komisen

**2:10.7–2:31.3** (N09) The long tenon goes deep into the sill. A hardwood pin, the komisen, is driven sideways through sill and tenon together. Where a tenon passes right through and sticks out, it can instead be pinned through that projecting part: a hanasen.

**2:31.7–2:48.4** (N10) The holes are made so the pin draws the two members together: the hole through the tenon is bored slightly closer to the shoulder than the hole through the sill. Driving the pin pulls the post down tight onto the sill.

**2:48.8–3:09.7** (N11) The public-works specification asks for square holes and pins; one lab report notes round pins are now more common. In a bearing wall, the Flat 35 specification puts a pinned long tenon in the same class as an L-shaped metal corner plate.


## 6. Cutting the tenon

**3:13.9–3:29.2** (N12) Cutting the tenon: rip down the cheeks first, then crosscut the shoulder, with the kerf on the waste side of its line. The public-works specification warns: don't saw the crosscuts too deep.

**3:29.6–3:41.2** (N13) In the skills-test manual's practice piece, a beam's tenon is cut a little loose in thickness and a little tight in width, as we saw in Episode 4.


## 7. Chopping the mortise

**3:41.9–3:57.2** (N14) Chopping the mortise: use a mortise chisel a little narrower than the hole. Start a few millimeters inside the lines, rough it out, then pare back to the lines and check the depth.

**3:57.6–4:06.3** (N15) The manual adds: strike lightly when the edge runs along the grain, and a little harder when it cuts across it.


## 8. Fit

**4:09.5–4:28.6** (N16) Fit it in small steps. In the manual's practice piece, the width goes in with light taps over a block of wood; the thickness, by hand. For posts, the public-works specification asks for a snug fit: driven in, and fully seated.


## 9. Next time

**4:31.3–4:35.6** (N17) Next time: where one beam crosses over another.


## 10. Recap and a note

**4:36.6–4:49.4** (N18) A short tenon needs hardware to hold. A long tenon can be pinned so it pulls itself tight. And a snug fit, made in small steps, does the rest.

**4:59.3–5:09.8** (N19) One note: sharp-tool technique, and how to use these tools safely, is best learned from a teacher, at a bench, in person.
