| S03 Hozo and hozo-ana | 0:25.8 | 33.7 s | 2d | Tenon and mortise labelled; older reading; a stepped tenon at a post top (schematic). Study hold 2.5 s. | Hozo is the tenon, the tongue. Hozo-ana, literally tenon hole, is the mortise. Dictionaries note an older reading, hoso. There are many named kinds. A stepped tenon, the jūhozo, or stacked tenon, for example, goes at the top of a post where a wall plate and a beam stack up. This episode looks at the joint where a post meets a sill. |

| S05 The short tenon | 1:32.0 | 38.4 s | threeQ | Cutaway: 105 mm post and sill, tenon 30 × 85 × 50 (howtec test standard); bearing-wall rating card (neutral). Study hold 3.5 s. | How big is a short tenon? The test standard for Japan's certified joint hardware uses one on a hundred-and-five millimeter post and sill: thirty thick, eighty-five wide, fifty deep. In a bearing wall, Japan's standard detail drawings give a plain short tenon a joint rating of zero: it isn't counted on to stop the post pulling out. That job needs rated hardware, or a pinned long tenon. |
| S06 The long tenon and its pin | 2:10.4 | 63.2 s | threeQ | Cutaway on 120 mm members (Toda 2015): long tenon, square pin; hanasen schematic; offset-hole inset (exaggerated). Study hold 3.5 s. | The long tenon goes deep into the sill. A hardwood pin, the komisen, is driven sideways through sill and tenon together. Where a tenon passes right through and sticks out, it can instead be pinned through that projecting part: a hanasen. The holes are made so the pin draws the two members together: the hole through the tenon is bored slightly closer to the shoulder than the hole through the sill. Driving the pin pulls the post down tight onto the sill. The public-works specification asks for square holes and pins; one lab report notes round pins are now more common. In a bearing wall, the Flat 35 specification puts a pinned long tenon in the same class as an L-shaped metal corner plate. |
| S07 Cutting the tenon | 3:13.6 | 28.0 s | threeQ | Rip the cheeks, crosscut the shoulder (kerf on the waste side); ✕ inset: a crosscut sawn too deep. | Cutting the tenon: rip down the cheeks first, then crosscut the shoulder, with the kerf on the waste side of its line. The public-works specification warns: don't saw the crosscuts too deep. In the skills-test manual's practice piece, a beam's tenon is cut a little loose in thickness and a little tight in width, as we saw in Episode 4. |
| S08 Chopping the mortise | 3:41.6 | 27.5 s | threeQ | Mortise chisel narrower than the hole; start inside the lines; rough out; pare to the lines; check depth. Study hold 2.5 s. | Chopping the mortise: use a mortise chisel a little narrower than the hole. Start a few millimeters inside the lines, rough it out, then pare back to the lines and check the depth. The manual adds: strike lightly when the edge runs along the grain, and a little harder when it cuts across it. |
| S09 Fit | 4:09.2 | 21.8 s | threeQ | The tenon tapped in over a block; snug, driven in and seated. Study hold 2.0 s. | Fit it in small steps. In the manual's practice piece, the width goes in with light taps over a block of wood; the thickness, by hand. For posts, the public-works specification asks for a snug fit: driven in, and fully seated. |
