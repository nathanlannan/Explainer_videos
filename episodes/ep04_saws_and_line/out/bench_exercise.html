Bench exercise: take, leave, split

Japanese Joinery by Hand · Episode 4 · Part 1. Cut to the same line three ways and measure what each choice does to the piece. Any fine crosscut saw works: a ryōba's crosscut edge (横挽き), a dōzuki, or a Western backsaw. About 45 minutes.

Safety. Clamp the work; never hold it with your hand in line with the cut. Start the kerf with a few light strokes. Sharp-tool technique is best learned from a teacher, at a bench, in person.

1 · Prepare

  1. Take a planed board about 20 × 90 mm, at least 400 mm long, with one straight, square end.
  2. From that end, mark three lines across the face at 100 mm, 200 mm and 300 mm, squared from the same reference edge. Use a sharp pencil or a knife, and keep the lines fine and even.
  3. Mark the waste side of each line with an ✕: the side away from the end you measured from.

2 · Cut three ways

  1. Leave the line at 100 mm: saw entirely on the waste side, so the whole line stays on the piece.
  2. Split the line at 200 mm: keep the kerf centered on the line, so a sliver remains on each side.
  3. Take the line at 300 mm: saw so the kerf just removes the line from the piece.

Cut each piece off and number it 1, 2, 3.

Measure each offcut's length with a rule or calipers. Leave should be the longest. Split and take differ by only a fraction of a millimetre, and which is shorter depends on whether your kerf is wider than your line. Measure your kerf and your line width and see if that explains it.

4 · Look at the teeth

  1. Look along your saw's teeth with a lamp behind. Can you see the set (asari), the teeth bent out alternately?
  2. If you have a ryōba, compare its two edges: rip teeth like small chisels, crosscut teeth like small knives.

What you should have

  • Three pieces, the leave piece longest, and an explanation of the other two from your kerf and line widths.
  • A feel for which choice you'd use for a snug fit, and which for a looser one.
