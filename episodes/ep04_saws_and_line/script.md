# Episode 4 · Part 1 — Saws and the Line · final script

## 1. Cold open

**0:00.6–0:08.3** (N01) A Japanese saw cuts as you pull it toward you. That one fact shapes much of the tool.

**0:08.7–0:15.5** (N02) This time: the saws, and exactly where the cut goes in relation to the line.


## 2. Why pull?

**0:21.3–0:37.1** (N03) Most Western saws cut on the push stroke. Japanese saws cut on the pull. Pulling puts the blade in tension, so it can be made thinner. A pushed blade generally needs to be stiffer, so that it doesn't buckle.

**0:37.5–0:47.6** (N04) Every saw cut has a width, called the kerf. A thinner blade usually makes a narrower kerf, so there is less wood to remove.


## 3. The ryōba and its teeth

**0:51.8–1:14.0** (N05) A common carpenter's saw is the ryōba, which means double-edged. One edge rips, cutting along the grain; that's tatebiki. The other crosscuts, cutting across it; that's yokobiki. The design is fairly modern: it dates from the Meiji era, in the late eighteen hundreds.

**1:17.7–1:32.9** (N06) Look closely at the teeth. Rip teeth work like a row of tiny chisels, paring along the fibers. Crosscut teeth are pointed like small knives, and sever the fibers one by one.


## 4. The dōzuki and the kataba

**1:37.1–1:48.2** (N07) For fine joinery there is the dōzuki. Its blade is very thin, so a metal spine along the back keeps it stiff. It is classed as a crosscut saw.

**1:48.6–2:01.4** (N08) Dōzuki is also the word for a tenon's shoulder, the face around its base. Cutting it is a clean crosscut: exactly the kind of work this saw does.

**2:05.0–2:10.6** (N09) There is also the kataba, a saw with a single edge and no spine.


## 5. Kerf and set

**2:13.3–2:29.7** (N10) Look at the kerf end-on. The teeth are bent out alternately, left and right. This is called the set, or asari. So the kerf is a little wider than the blade, and that width is why the next question matters.


## 6. Cutting to the line

**2:33.9–2:56.4** (N11) Where does the kerf go, relative to the line? The carpenter chooses, and the skills test manual sets out three options. In English shop talk: you can take the line, cutting it away; leave the line, sawing just on the waste side; or split it, with the kerf centered on the line.

**2:56.8–3:04.7** (N12) The choice depends on how tight each joint should be. Some fits should be snug, and some a little looser.

**3:09.4–3:28.5** (N13) The national skills test manual for carpenters gives an example. On one tenon, the thickness cuts split the line, so it fits a little looser. The width cuts leave the line, with the saw just on the waste side, so it fits tight.

**3:28.9–3:35.0** (N14) Either way, the line is the reference. That's why the marking out had to be exact.


## 7. Western cousins

**3:39.2–3:50.9** (N15) If you own Western saws, the same ideas carry over: rip and crosscut teeth, a backed saw for joinery. The big difference is the stroke.


## 8. Next time

**3:53.7–3:59.3** (N16) Next time, the rest of the kit: the chisels, the hammer, and the plane.


## 9. Recap and a note

**4:00.3–4:13.1** (N17) Pull to cut. Rip along the grain, crosscut across it. And before every cut, decide: take the line, leave it, or split it.

**4:23.0–4:32.6** (N18) One note: sharp-tool technique, and how to use these tools safely, is best learned from a teacher, at a bench, in person.
