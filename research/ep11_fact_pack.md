**Avoid:**
- "The upper piece slides in from the side" (J, TK). Three independent sources (住吉・松井 via KB, 下山, the denmoku bending sheet) say it is lowered from above along the slope. 下山 says the *kanawa* is the one assembled from the side.
- "The pegs draw the joint tight" or "drawbore". The sliding slope draws the shoulders tight as the piece is lowered. The pegs lock it against lifting out and shear slip.
- "The pegs carry the tension." Engineering sources ignore them in tension design. In one series pegs raised Pmax only modestly (R11-19).
- "As strong as one piece", "rigid" or "moment connection". It is semi-rigid: better than kama in bending, but usually modelled as a pin.
- Drawing it as a horizontal half-lap (one piece on top of the other with the scarf seen from the side). In the drawings the stepped line shows on the top face and the pegs on the side faces.
- Calling the pieces male and female as if different. Both are the same shape, turned end for end. 上木/下木 is the order of assembly.
- Mixing up 顎 (the centre step with the slope) and 目違 (the small end tongues). W calls the end housing 襟輪.
- A single "standard" slope. Sources give 1/10 (W) and 1/20 of depth (住吉・松井).
- "Splice length = 3× depth" as engineering fact. It is a carpenters' rule (W, 住吉・松井). Engineering sheets suggest 1.5–3×.
- A standard peg size from W's 五分 / 六分 lines (ambiguous). Use "hardwood, about 15–24 mm square or round" only with attribution.
- "Okkake is X times stronger than kama" as a rule. Give numbers only with specimen context (R11-32 to R11-35).
- "A longer splice is proportionally stronger." Depth matters more.
- Saying MLIT specifies it. MLIT leaves splice shape to 特記.
- Saying Flat 35 requires hardware on the okkake splice, or that Flat 35 allows it for sills, 間仕切りげた, 母屋 or 大引き.
- Saying the miyashita blog's round pegs driven from above.
- Explaining the name 追掛け ("chasing"). No source explains it.
- "Dates from about 1400" stated as fact (one thesis citing 内田, hedged 「と思われる」).
- おっかけだいせんづぎ / "okkake daisen-zugi".
